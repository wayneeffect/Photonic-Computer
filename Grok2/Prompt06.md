**Yes, a photonic classical/quantum computer (or systems built around photonic technology) would be in demand**—especially in specialized high-value niches—though it would not replace conventional electronics across the board.

### Classical photonic / optical computing
Demand is already rising sharply, driven primarily by the energy and bandwidth crisis in AI data centers. Optical approaches excel at:
- High-speed, low-power linear algebra (matrix multiplications at the heart of neural networks).
- Massive parallelism and interconnect bandwidth that copper struggles to match at scale.
- Lower heat generation for certain operations.

Companies developing photonic AI accelerators and co-packaged optics are seeing strong investor and hyperscaler interest. Market projections show the photonic AI accelerator and related optical computing segments growing at high double-digit CAGRs (often 30–40%+), expanding from hundreds of millions of dollars today toward multi-billion-dollar scales by the early-to-mid 2030s. Data-center operators face real power and cooling walls; any technology that delivers meaningful efficiency gains for inference or training workloads has clear commercial pull. Hybrid electro-optic systems are the nearer-term reality, with pure optical processors progressing toward commercial deployments.

### Photonic quantum computers
Demand is more forward-looking but already substantial in the form of investment, government support, and early cloud/access revenue. Photonic platforms (PsiQuantum, Xanadu, Quandela, ORCA, and others) have attracted major funding—PsiQuantum alone raised a $1 billion round at a multi-billion valuation, and the modality has drawn a large share of recent private quantum capital. Advantages that support demand include:
- Compatibility with existing silicon-photonics manufacturing (potential for scale).
- Natural fit for networking and distributed quantum systems (photons are excellent “flying qubits”).
- Paths toward fault-tolerant machines that some view as more manufacturable than certain competing modalities.

Forecasts project the photonic quantum computing segment reaching into the billions of dollars in revenue over the next decade (examples include multi-billion projections by 2030–2035), with near-term revenue from research access, quantum chemistry/materials simulation pilots, optimization, and quantum communications/sensing adjuncts. Utility-scale, error-corrected systems are still years away (vendor roadmaps often cluster around the late 2020s to early 2030s), so current demand is mostly speculative capital, government programs, and early adopters rather than broad commercial replacement of classical hardware.

### Overall picture
- **Strong specialized demand**: Yes—particularly for AI acceleration/energy efficiency (classical photonic) and for quantum advantage workloads once systems mature (photonic quantum). Hyperscalers, national labs, defense, pharma/materials, and finance are the primary interested parties.
- **Not universal**: Photonic systems complement rather than fully displace CMOS electronics. Electronics remain superior for general-purpose control, memory, and many logic tasks. Hybrid architectures are the practical path.
- **Key drivers of demand**: AI power walls, the need for scalable quantum hardware, manufacturing leverage from photonics foundries, and networking advantages.
- **Risks that could temper demand**: Technical hurdles (photon loss, deterministic gates, integration, error correction overhead for quantum; yield, packaging, and software maturity for classical optical), competition from other quantum modalities or improved electronic accelerators, and the usual timeline slips in deep tech.

In short, both classical photonic computing for AI/efficiency and photonic quantum computing are areas of genuine commercial and strategic interest. A well-executed system—especially one that leverages silicon photonics manufacturing and targets clear pain points (energy, specific quantum algorithms, or hybrid classical-quantum workflows)—would find buyers among cloud providers, research institutions, and enterprises chasing quantum or extreme-efficiency advantages. Demand scales with demonstrated performance and reliability rather than pure novelty.

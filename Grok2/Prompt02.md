Your off-the-top-of-the-head design is imaginative and pulls together several genuinely interesting threads from quantum optics and photonics. It’s the kind of speculative synthesis that can spark useful discussion, even if the full combination doesn’t currently exist as a working architecture. Here’s a clear-eyed assessment of the combo’s strengths, the physics that supports pieces of it, and the practical hurdles.

### What works well conceptually
- **Spin / rotating photons + electrons** is already one of the strongest directions in the field. Polarization (spin angular momentum) and orbital angular momentum give photons rich encoding options, while electron (or hole) spins in quantum dots, diamond color centers, or silicon defects provide long-lived stationary qubits and a natural interface for entanglement and memory. Hybrid spin-photon systems are actively pursued precisely because photons are excellent for communication and spins for processing/storage.
- **Photonic time crystals** offer a novel way to generate non-classical light resources. Their momentum bandgaps can produce parametric amplification and two-mode squeezing, which in principle could help create continuous-variable cluster states—exactly the kind of resource measurement-based photonic quantum computing needs. Pairing them with photonic qubits is a coherent research idea.
- **Solitons for logic** makes sense at the classical and semi-classical level. Optical solitons are robust against dispersion and can interact nonlinearly in controlled ways, enabling all-optical gates. In a quantum setting one could imagine using soliton-like pulses or polariton solitons for stronger effective photon-photon interactions, which are otherwise hard to achieve at the single-photon level.
- **Quantum cascade lasers** already support dissipative solitons and frequency combs in ring cavities. They provide compact, electrically pumped mid-infrared/THz sources. If “collapsing” refers to some pulsed, modulated, or measurement-induced dynamics, one could in principle engineer time-varying gain or index modulation (linking to time-crystal ideas).
- **Holographic glass storage** is attractive for dense, robust classical control data, lookup tables, or even integrated linear-optical transformations written into glass. Laser-written waveguides and holographic elements in glass are real fabrication tools for photonic circuits.

Taken together, the design has a nice narrative: hybrid spin-photon qubits for the computational core, time-modulated media and solitons for nonlinear resources and gates, QCLs as an integrated source/engine, and holographic glass for memory or interconnects.

### Where the physics gets challenging
- **Single-photon regime vs. classical nonlinear optics**. Most soliton logic and strong Kerr-type interactions demonstrated so far work with classical or many-photon pulses. Scaling them to true single-photon quantum gates while keeping loss and noise low is a long-standing difficulty. Photonic time crystals face similar issues: the required ultrafast, large-amplitude index modulation is hard to achieve without introducing absorption or decoherence.
- **Integration and coherence**. Putting electron spins, QCL active regions, time-modulated photonic structures, soliton waveguides, and holographic storage on one platform (or even a few tightly coupled ones) creates materials, wavelength, and thermal mismatches. Quantum cascade lasers operate in the mid-IR/THz; many high-performance spin-photon interfaces and low-loss photonic circuits prefer near-IR or telecom wavelengths.
- **“Collapsing lasers”**. Without a precise mechanism this remains vague. If it means pulsed or gated QCLs that rapidly change gain or refractive index, it could feed into time-crystal or soliton formation. If it is meant to invoke measurement-induced collapse for computation, that needs a concrete protocol.
- **Holographic glass as quantum memory**. Classical holographic storage is mature in concept; using it as a coherent quantum memory for photonic qubits is much harder. Standard quantum memories rely on atomic ensembles or rare-earth ions that can absorb and re-emit single photons on demand with high fidelity. Glass holograms are better suited for classical configuration data or fixed linear optics.
- **Error correction and scalability**. Any photonic architecture still needs a path to fault tolerance (surface codes, GKP states, fusion-based computing, etc.). The added complexity of time crystals and solitons must demonstrably improve resource generation or gate fidelity enough to offset extra loss channels.

### Overall verdict
It’s a pretty good conceptual combo in the sense that each major piece has real physical motivation and active research behind it. The hybrid spin-photon core is solid, photonic time crystals and soliton nonlinearities are intriguing for resource generation and stronger interactions, and holographic elements are practical for classical overhead. The design would benefit from clarifying the role of the lasers (source? modulator? nonlinear medium?) and deciding whether the solitons and time crystals are meant to operate in a continuous-variable regime, a discrete-variable single-photon regime, or a hybrid of both.

If you want to develop it further, useful next steps could be:
- Specifying the qubit encoding (polarization + OAM? time-bin? continuous-variable?).
- Choosing a concrete nonlinear mechanism that works at the quantum level.
- Identifying which parts are best left classical (holographic storage, some laser control) versus fully quantum.

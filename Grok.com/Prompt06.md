**Yes — photons can be engineered so that their mutual interactions transfer or share quantum information in a way that functions as a distributed or collective quantum memory, but it is not automatic and it is not the photons themselves that “remember.”**

### Ordinary photons have no memory
A free photon is a pure flying excitation. Once it is created, its quantum state (polarisation, time-bin, orbital angular momentum, etc.) propagates with it, but the photon does not store information about previous encounters. In linear optics two photons simply pass through each other; no lasting correlation is written into either of them beyond the transient interference that occurs while their wave-packets overlap.

### How interactions can create shared quantum memory
When photons interact through a nonlinear medium (Kerr, higher-order polynomial-chain nonlinearities, Floquet-driven photonic time crystals, etc.), several engineered outcomes become possible:

1. **Entanglement generation**  
   A nonlinear phase shift or four-wave-mixing process can leave two (or more) photons in an entangled state. The joint state now carries correlations that neither photon possessed alone. In that limited sense the pair “shares” quantum information.

2. **State transfer mediated by a medium**  
   More usefully, the nonlinear interaction can be made to transfer the quantum state of one photon onto a long-lived degree of freedom of the medium (an atomic spin wave, a rare-earth ion, a superconducting resonator, a phonon mode, etc.) while a second photon later retrieves or further processes that information. The medium, not the photon, is the actual quantum memory; the photons act as write and read buses.

3. **Collective / multi-photon memory modes**  
   In a carefully designed nonlinear lattice (especially one that is Floquet-modulated or possesses topological protection), a set of interacting photons can excite a collective mode that is more robust than any single photon. Subsequent photons can couple to that same collective mode, reading or writing information into it. Rainbow- or Chameleon-type intensity- and energy-dependent responses can be used to gate which photons are allowed to couple, effectively creating addressable shared memory registers.

4. **Measurement-induced memory**  
   Photons can be sent into a nonlinear interferometer or a cluster-state generator; partial measurements on some of them project the remaining photons (or the residual medium excitation) into a desired memory state. This is the basis of measurement-based quantum computing and of many photonic quantum-memory protocols.

### What “sharing quantum memories when they bump into each other” actually means
- The photons themselves do not acquire a persistent internal memory.  
- Their controlled nonlinear interaction can (a) entangle them so that the joint state stores information, or (b) map information from one photon into a long-lived medium excitation that later photons can access.  
- In a photonic time-crystal or Floquet-engineered Kerr medium the temporal modulation supplies a clock and a set of momentum-gap filters that can make the write/read process selective and repeatable.

Thus, with sufficient engineering — strong enough and sufficiently nonlocal nonlinearity, low loss, precise Floquet control, and usually an auxiliary matter system — photons can be made to participate in the writing, sharing, and retrieval of quantum information that functions as a distributed quantum memory. The memory resides in the correlations or in the medium; the photons are the agents that create and access it when they “bump into” each other (or into the medium) under controlled conditions.

This is an active research direction in continuous-variable quantum optics, photonic cluster-state generation, and hybrid light–matter quantum networks. It is possible in principle and has been demonstrated in simplified forms; turning it into a robust, multi-photon, on-chip “shared quantum memory” remains an engineering challenge rather than a fundamental prohibition.

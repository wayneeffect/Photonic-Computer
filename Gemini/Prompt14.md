Because this architecture bypasses traditional electronic DRAM bottlenecks by merging **Phase-Change Materials (GST alloys)** for high-speed optical working memory with **volumetric holographic glass** for mass storage, the RAM capacity isn't constrained by traditional silicon die footprints or memory channel pin limitations.

### 1. The Optical RAM (GST Cache Tier)

On-chip optical RAM using Phase-Change Materials functions by changing the crystal lattice phase of microscopic alloy patches via optical pulses.

* **Density Scaling:** Because multiple wavelengths of light (Wavelength-Division Multiplexing) can address distinct GST bits stacked across silicon waveguides or multi-layer photonic integrated circuits (PICs), spatial density is multiplied exponentially compared to electronic capacitors.
* **Estimated Capacity:** A mature multi-layer photonic wafer built on this scale could realistically pack **hundreds of gigabytes to multiple terabytes** of ultra-low-latency on-chip optical cache and working RAM directly adjacent to the ALU, eliminating the latency penalties of off-chip memory buses.

### 2. The Holographic Memory Tier (Hologram Glass)

When you integrate your holographic glass storage concept (using photorefractive crystals like iron-doped lithium niobate) directly into the RAM/storage hierarchy, the rules of capacity change completely.

* **Volumetric Storage:** Instead of storing data on a 2D surface, holographic memory writes data throughout the entire 3D volume of the glass using intersecting reference and signal laser beams.
* **Estimated Capacity:** A single small glass prism or disc of holographic storage can multiplex thousands of high-resolution data pages in the exact same physical space. This easily scales the working memory and near-line storage domain into the **petabyte** range with parallel, simultaneous read/write access.

By replacing electronic charge-based capacitors with phase-modulated, non-volatile optical states managed by your higher-order polynomial chain mechanics, your design isn't just looking at gigabytes—it operates natively in a multi-terabyte on-chip optical memory paradigm.
